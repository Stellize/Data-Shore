# Waves at the Edge

*Notes on reading a shoreline that refuses to hold still*

There are two ways to keep an archive of a coast. The first is to photograph it, which is a
way of saying: *this is how it looked, and I was here to see it.* The second is to measure it,
which is a way of admitting that the photograph was already out of date.

The Tide Atlas tries to do both, badly and honestly.

## 1. The table is a promise

A tide table is not a description of the sea. It is a promise the moon makes to a harbour
master, written down in advance so that boats can be loaded and nets mended. When the table
says *low water at 06:12*, it means: **be there at 06:12, and the sea will have done its part.**

Most of the time, it has. The table's usefulness comes from that *most*.

> The sea does not read the table. The table reads the sea, once, and then repeats itself
> politely for a year.

## 2. Fourteen anemones

In March 2024 we counted fourteen open anemones in a pool that should have been asleep. The
guidebook says they close in winter. The guidebook was written from another coast, by people
who had not met these particular anemones.

This is the small, ordinary scandal of field notes: the living thing declines to match the
description. A careful archive should record the disagreement, not correct it.

## 3. What the marsh writes

From the ridge, the marsh creek looks like handwriting. It is not — it is water finding the
easiest way through ground that is mostly root and rot. But the resemblance is stubborn, and
the creek rewrites itself after every high tide.

We photographed it at dawn for three years. No two photographs are the same, and none of them
are wrong.

## 4. On keeping a record

An archive is a promise too: that something was here, that someone looked, that the looking
was worth the trouble of writing down. It does not need to be complete. It needs to be honest,
and it needs to be **readable** — which is why this one is published as a plain, quiet,
read-only shelf rather than a database.

If you are reading this years from now: the coast has moved. The photographs have not.
Trust both.

— *Lampang, March 2024*
